Dutch citizenship in 2026: naturalisation rules and a looming change

9 min read  ·  Last updated July 2026
By the Expat in Holland editorial team  ·  Reviewed June 2026 against official sources

Becoming a Dutch citizen has, for decades, followed a fairly stable rule: five years of uninterrupted legal residence, alongside proof of integration, was usually enough to apply. That baseline is now under review. In late 2025 the Dutch cabinet published a draft bill that would extend the standard naturalisation period from five years to ten, and by mid-2026 the proposal was working its way through parliament rather than sitting on a shelf. For expats weighing whether and when to apply for Dutch citizenship, understanding both the current rules and the direction of travel matters.

What naturalisation requires today

Under the rules still in force in 2026, most applicants can apply for naturalisation after five consecutive years of legal residence in the Netherlands. You also need to satisfy the civic integration requirement — either the inburgering exam or an approved diploma exemption — and demonstrate Dutch language ability, currently assessed at A2 level for most applicants. Spouses and registered partners of Dutch nationals benefit from a shorter route: three years of residence and cohabitation is generally sufficient, and in some cases naturalisation can even be arranged from abroad. Dual nationality is not automatically permitted; most applicants must renounce their existing nationality, subject to a list of exemptions that includes people who cannot renounce under the law of their country of origin.

The proposed extension to ten years

The bill put forward by the Ministry of Justice and Security would raise the standard residence requirement from five years to ten. The stated aim, according to State Secretary Arno Rutte, is to ensure applicants have built deeper and more durable ties to the Netherlands before acquiring citizenship. The spousal route would also change: the three-year period for partners of Dutch nationals would rise to five years, and naturalisation from abroad for spouses and former Dutch nationals would be abolished entirely. The language bar is also expected to rise, from A2 to B1, regardless of how the residence-period debate concludes.

Other changes buried in the draft

Beyond the headline five-to-ten change, the bill closes a route some applicants have used to hold dual nationality: renouncing Dutch citizenship shortly after naturalising, then reclaiming it a year later through the option procedure for former Dutch nationals, which does not carry the same renunciation requirement. Under the new bill, anyone who reclaims citizenship this way would face the renunciation requirement again, even retroactively for applications not yet decided. The bill would also remove an existing exception that lets some long-term residents naturalise after just two uninterrupted years, provided they have accumulated ten years of lawful residence in total. If adopted as drafted, that shortcut disappears.

Where the proposal stands

The bill went through public internet consultation until 1 December 2025 and has since been reviewed by the Council of State before formal submission to the Tweede Kamer (House of Representatives). Parliamentary planning documents pointed to a plenary debate in the first quarter of 2026, though timelines for legislation of this kind routinely slip. Crucially, the proposal originated under a caretaker cabinet before the general election of 29 October 2025, so its ultimate fate depends on the priorities of the coalition that followed. As of mid-2026, the current five-year rule remains the law — nothing has changed yet for anyone applying today.

The bill is not expected to apply retroactively for the core five-to-ten change. Applications submitted, and naturalisation procedures already under way, before any new law takes effect would continue to be assessed under the existing five-year rule. That detail is the single most consequential piece of information for anyone currently eligible or close to eligible.

What this means if you are working toward citizenship

If you have already passed the five-year mark, or expect to within the next year or two, it is worth treating your application as time-sensitive rather than assuming you can apply whenever suits you. Immigration solicitors have warned that a rush of applications is likely as the bill progresses, which could extend waiting times for integration exams and naturalisation appointments in the larger cities, particularly Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and The Hague. If you are relying on the shorter spousal route, or on the ten-year residence exception that the bill would abolish, the case for applying sooner rather than later is stronger still. Anyone with a gap in their residence permit history — a so-called residence gap — should also treat the proposal as a reason to resolve it now, since an uninterrupted record becomes more valuable the longer the required period gets. Applying for a permanent residence permit at the five-year mark is worth considering even if you are not yet ready to naturalise, since it prevents a residence gap forming while you wait.

Getting advice

Because the rules are in flux and the consequences of missing a deadline are real, this is an area where a short consultation with an immigration solicitor is worth the cost for anyone close to the threshold. Dutch immigration law firms have been tracking the bill closely and can advise on residence gaps, the option procedure, and whether it makes sense to apply now under the current rules rather than wait. For expats who are years away from meeting even the current five-year threshold, the practical takeaway is simpler: keep your residence permit continuous, keep records of your address history, and revisit the question once the bill's progress through parliament becomes clearer.

Naturalisation or option: two different routes

Dutch citizenship is acquired through one of two procedures, and applicants regularly pursue the wrong one.

Naturalisation is the general route: the residence period, the integration exam, the language requirement, and in most cases renunciation of your existing nationality. It is discretionary, takes up to a year to decide, and carries a substantial fee.

The option procedure is a faster, cheaper declaration available to defined groups — people born in the Netherlands who have lived there since birth, certain long-term residents who arrived as minors, former Dutch nationals, and some partners in specific circumstances. Where you qualify it is markedly simpler and decided in a matter of months rather than a year.

Because the criteria are narrow and technical, it is worth checking whether you fall inside the option procedure before assuming naturalisation is your only path — particularly if you arrived as a child or previously held Dutch nationality.

What renunciation actually means in practice

The requirement to give up your existing nationality is the part applicants most underestimate, because it is not a formality on a Dutch form — it is a separate legal process with your own country, conducted on their timetable and at their cost.

Exemptions exist and are broader than commonly assumed. They generally cover people whose country does not permit renunciation at all, refugees, spouses of Dutch nationals, those born in the Netherlands, and cases where renunciation would cause disproportionate harm — losing inheritance rights or property ownership in the country of origin, for instance. Whether your situation falls inside an exemption is genuinely case-specific.

Think through the consequences before starting. Losing your original nationality can affect the right to live and work there, property and inheritance rights, visa-free travel on that passport, and your children's citizenship. These are not reversible on a change of mind.

Cost, timing and the ceremony

Naturalisation carries a substantial application fee — several hundred euros for a single applicant, more for couples, with reduced rates for children applying alongside a parent. It is payable up front and is not refunded if your application is refused, which is a strong argument for confirming eligibility before filing.

Applications are submitted at your gemeente, which forwards them to the IND. The statutory decision period runs up to a year and can be extended. Once approved, citizenship is not acquired until you attend a naturalisation ceremony and make the declaration of solidarity — municipalities hold these periodically, and missing yours delays acquisition until the next one.

Keeping the residence clock intact

The residence requirement demands uninterrupted legal residence, and that is a technical condition rather than a loose one. Extended absences can break it, gaps between residence permits can break it, and a lapse in municipal registration can call the whole period into question.

Two habits protect you. Keep continuous registration and continuous valid residence status, renewing permits well before expiry rather than at the edge. And keep your own records — permit copies, registration history, evidence of presence — because you may be asked to demonstrate a period the authorities cannot fully reconstruct themselves.

If a long posting abroad is likely, take advice before you go. Some absences are tolerated and others reset the clock, and the difference is worth knowing in advance rather than discovering at application.

Frequently asked questions

How many years of residence do I need for Dutch citizenship right now?

Five years of continuous legal residence remains the standard requirement today. A bill has been proposed to extend this to ten years, but a proposal is not law — until it is actually passed and in force, applications continue to be assessed under the current five-year rule.

Should I apply now or wait to see what happens with the ten-year proposal?

If you already meet today's requirements, there is a clear argument for not delaying, since applications are assessed under the rules in force when you apply. If you are still some years from eligibility, the timing question is genuinely uncertain and worth raising with an immigration lawyer rather than guessing — this guide cannot tell you how the bill will land.

Do I have to give up my current nationality?

The Netherlands restricts dual nationality, and renunciation of your existing citizenship is required in many cases, though exemptions exist for certain circumstances. Because the exemptions are specific and the consequences permanent, confirm how the rules apply to your own nationality with the IND before starting an application.

What's the difference between naturalisation and the option procedure?

Naturalisation is the general route — residence period, integration exam, language requirement, usually renunciation — and takes up to a year. The option procedure is a faster, cheaper declaration open to defined groups, including people born in the Netherlands, some who arrived as minors, and former Dutch nationals. Check whether you qualify for option before assuming naturalisation is your only path.

What does giving up my original nationality actually involve?

A separate legal process with your own country, on their timetable and at their cost — not a Dutch formality. Exemptions are broader than people assume, covering countries that don't permit renunciation, refugees, spouses of Dutch nationals, and cases of disproportionate harm such as losing inheritance or property rights. Consider the knock-on effects on residence, property and your children's status first; they aren't reversible.

Can a period abroad break my residence clock?

Yes. The requirement is uninterrupted legal residence, and extended absences, gaps between permits, or a lapse in municipal registration can all jeopardise it. Renew permits well before expiry, keep registration continuous, and keep your own records. If a long posting abroad is coming, take advice before you go rather than at application.
